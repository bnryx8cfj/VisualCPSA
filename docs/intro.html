CPSA DSL Reference

Introduction

Purpose of This Reference

This document is a publication-quality reference specification for the Cryptographic Protocol Shapes Analyzer (CPSA) Version 4 Domain Specific Language (DSL). It serves two related purposes: first, as a language reference manual for CPSA 4 input specifications, and second, as a UML metamodel specification for CPSA DSL constructs (Liskov, Ramsdell, Guttman, & Rowe, 2026; Guttman & Ramsdell, 2026).

The reference describes CPSA declarations, syntax, semantics, relationships, constraints, processing behavior, and modeling constructs with sufficient precision to support both human readers and automated authoring systems. Those topics are treated as normative where they define the DSL and informative where they provide background, guidance, examples, or rationale (Liskov et al., 2026; CPSA DSL Reference Requirements, 2026).

CPSA Overview

The Cryptographic Protocol Shapes Analyzer is a symbolic protocol-analysis tool designed to enumerate the essentially different executions possible for a cryptographic protocol. CPSA calls these executions shapes, and the shape set is used to inspect authentication properties, secrecy properties, attacks, and anomalous protocol behavior (MITRE, 2026; Liskov et al., 2026).

CPSA analyzes an input problem by starting from an initial description of protocol behavior and discovering the compatible shapes. This initial behavior is often stated from the perspective of one participant, and the analysis then identifies what other participants, adversary actions, or environmental assumptions must be present for that behavior to occur (MITRE, 2026; Liskov et al., 2026).

CPSA belongs to the symbolic-analysis tradition. In this tradition, cryptographic mechanisms are modeled algebraically rather than by computational reductions, and protocol reasoning focuses on the structure, origin, disclosure, and causal ordering of messages (Thayer Fábrega, Herzog, & Guttman, 1998; Guttman & Ramsdell, 2026).

Historical Background

CPSA emerged from research on strand spaces and symbolic cryptographic protocol analysis conducted by Joshua D. Guttman, John D. Ramsdell, Jon C. Herzog, Shaddin F. Doghmi, F. Javier Thayer, Paul D. Rowe, Moses D. Liskov, and collaborators (MITRE, 2026; Liskov et al., 2026).

The strand-space framework predates CPSA 4 and provides the conceptual basis for representing protocol executions as collections of message events linked by causal structure. A foundational strand-space paper defines a strand as a sequence of events and a strand space as a collection of strands equipped with a graph structure generated by causal interaction (Thayer Fábrega et al., 1998).

CPSA 4 extends earlier CPSA approaches by incorporating an implementation-oriented strand-space formalism with CPSA-specific treatment of messages, carried-by relations, predicates, skeletons, channels, facts, and rules. In the CPSA 4 foundation document, protocols are described as containing both roles and rules, and communication events may optionally occur on channels (Guttman & Ramsdell, 2026).

Strand Space Theory

CPSA models protocol behavior using strand-space concepts. The following terms provide the conceptual vocabulary used throughout this reference.

  • Event: A message transmission or reception occurrence within a protocol execution. Strand-space literature treats a strand as a sequence of events, and CPSA 4 refines this treatment by allowing communication events to be associated with optional channels (Thayer Fábrega et al., 1998; Guttman & Ramsdell, 2026).
  • Node: A position within a strand at which an event occurs. Nodes provide the points over which ordering, causality, origination, and reception constraints are stated in strand-space analysis (Thayer Fábrega et al., 1998; Guttman & Ramsdell, 2026).
  • Strand: An ordered sequence of events. It may represent behavior of an honest protocol role or adversary behavior in the broader strand-space model (Thayer Fábrega et al., 1998).
  • Bundle: A graph-like representation of a realized protocol execution in which strands and communication edges satisfy causal and message-delivery constraints. Bundles are part of the formal strand-space account of executions and correctness claims (Thayer Fábrega et al., 1998; Guttman & Ramsdell, 2026).
  • Skeleton: A partial description of protocol behavior supplied for analysis. In CPSA, a skeleton represents assumptions about a possible execution, and shape analysis searches for realized executions compatible with those assumptions (Liskov et al., 2026; Guttman & Ramsdell, 2026).
  • Shape: A realized execution that is minimal, in the CPSA sense, with respect to the input skeleton. CPSA attempts to enumerate all essentially different shapes compatible with the initial behavior (MITRE, 2026; Liskov et al., 2026).
  • Realized execution: An execution in which the required receptions and causal dependencies are explained by available transmissions, role behavior, and adversary capabilities. CPSA shape analysis uses realization as a central condition for determining whether a skeleton has become a complete enough execution description (Liskov et al., 2026; Guttman & Ramsdell, 2026).

Related Concepts

Symbolic protocol analysis abstracts from bit-level cryptographic computation and instead models messages as terms governed by algebraic constructors, destructors, and inference capabilities. CPSA 4 follows this style through message algebras such as basic and diffie-hellman (Liskov et al., 2026; Guttman & Ramsdell, 2026).

The adversary model associated with strand-space and CPSA-style reasoning is closely related to the Dolev-Yao tradition: the adversary may synthesize, decompose, intercept, redirect, and replay messages according to symbolic rules, but is not assumed to break ideal cryptographic primitives directly (Thayer Fábrega et al., 1998; Guttman & Ramsdell, 2026).

Authentication goals state what participant behavior or causal structure must exist if a given local behavior is observed. Secrecy goals state that values should not become available to the adversary under the modeled assumptions. CPSA shapes support these analyses because the completed shapes describe the essentially different executions compatible with an input skeleton (MITRE, 2026; Liskov et al., 2026).

Relationship to the DSL

The CPSA DSL is the concrete syntax used to encode protocol definitions, analysis assumptions, and logical goals for CPSA processing. The top-level declaration forms organize the reference because they correspond to the major conceptual domains of CPSA modeling: protocol behavior, execution assumptions, and security goals (Liskov et al., 2026; CPSA DSL Reference Requirements, 2026).

Strand-space or analysis concept CPSA DSL declaration Reference package
Protocol definition defprotocol Protocol Package
Participant role defrole Protocol Package
Protocol rule defrule Protocol Package
Partial execution defskeleton Analysis Package
Strand instance defstrand Analysis Package
Listener strand or observation point deflistener Analysis Package
Security goal defgoal Goal Package
Logical formula Formula forms subordinate to defgoal Goal Package

The top-level organization of this reference shall therefore follow the seven CPSA declaration constructs: defprotocol, defrole, defskeleton, defstrand, deflistener, defgoal, and defrule (Liskov et al., 2026; CPSA DSL Reference Requirements, 2026).

Declaration-Level Metamodel Overview

The CPSA declaration metamodel defines relationships among Protocol, Role, Rule, Skeleton, Strand, Listener, Goal, and Formula. This reference treats those metamodel elements as the canonical declaration-level classes required for the UML representation of the DSL (CPSA DSL Reference Requirements, 2026).

The canonical metamodel shall be defined once and reused throughout the declaration chapters. Each declaration chapter shall include an English metamodel interpretation, a PlantUML representation, and an SVG representation. The PlantUML and SVG diagrams shall represent the same classes, attributes, relationships, multiplicities, stereotypes, packages, highlighting semantics, and containment structure (CPSA DSL Reference Requirements, 2026).

Supported Message Algebras

This reference documents, at minimum, the basic and diffie-hellman message algebras. CPSA 4 documentation describes algebra features including generic messages, long-term keys, hashing, constants, bidirectional long-term keys, Diffie-Hellman modeling, and language extensions for declaring new operators (Liskov et al., 2026).

The basic algebra documentation shall cover at least the operators cat, enc, hash, pubk, privk, and ltk. The diffie-hellman algebra documentation shall cover at least the operators gen, exp, mul, rec, and one (CPSA DSL Reference Requirements, 2026; Liskov et al., 2026).

Each algebra section shall document available operators, algebra-specific semantics, expressiveness, modeling implications, analysis implications, and performance considerations. Those topics are required because algebra selection affects the messages CPSA can express and the symbolic reasoning cases that analysis must consider (CPSA DSL Reference Requirements, 2026; Liskov et al., 2026).

Intended Audience

This reference is intended for CPSA users, protocol analysts, security researchers, tool developers, DSL implementers, and AI authoring systems. It is written as a normative specification rather than a tutorial, although informative explanations and worked examples may be included to improve understanding (CPSA DSL Reference Requirements, 2026).

Normative Language

The keywords SHALL, SHOULD, MAY, MUST, and MUST NOT are used in their conventional specification sense. Statements that define language syntax, language semantics, metamodel structure, attributes, relationships, multiplicities, constraints, UML/OCL invariants, or processing semantics are normative unless explicitly marked informative (CPSA DSL Reference Requirements, 2026).

Informative statements include historical background, explanatory notes, examples, commentary, modeling guidance, best practices, and design rationale. If an informative statement conflicts with a normative statement, the normative statement takes precedence (CPSA DSL Reference Requirements, 2026).

Example Attribution Policy

Examples derived from CPSA documentation, CPSA research papers, or canonical protocol-analysis examples shall include inline citations and explanatory prose identifying their source or lineage. This includes examples inspired by CPSA manuals, CPSA sample inputs, strand-space literature, or published security-protocol research (CPSA DSL Reference Requirements, 2026; Liskov et al., 2026).

Examples based on the Needham-Schroeder family shall explicitly identify the Needham-Schroeder lineage. The original Needham-Schroeder paper introduced authentication protocols using conventional and public-key cryptography, while Lowe later demonstrated an attack on the Needham-Schroeder public-key authentication protocol; strand-space literature also uses Needham-Schroeder-Lowe examples to explain authentication and secrecy reasoning (Needham & Schroeder, 1978; Lowe, 1995; Thayer Fábrega et al., 1998).

When examples are simplified or adapted for exposition, the prose shall state that the example is illustrative and not a normative definition of the underlying protocol family. The surrounding text shall cite the CPSA manual, CPSA foundation paper, or research source from which the example or modeling pattern was adapted (CPSA DSL Reference Requirements, 2026; Liskov et al., 2026; Guttman & Ramsdell, 2026).

Source Basis and Traceability

Technical claims, grammar definitions, semantic descriptions, and metamodel definitions should be traceable to authoritative CPSA sources, including CPSA manuals, CPSA technical reports, CPSA source repositories, CPSA sample inputs, and published research by Guttman, Ramsdell, Rowe, Liskov, Thayer, Herzog, and collaborators (CPSA DSL Reference Requirements, 2026; MITRE, 2026; Liskov et al., 2026).

Inline citations shall be attached to significant assertions, paraphrases, direct quotations, examples, and explanatory claims. Direct quotations shall include a precise source citation and, when available, page or section information. Bibliographies shall use APA style and include complete URLs, DOI values, or ISBN values where applicable (CPSA DSL Reference Requirements, 2026).
